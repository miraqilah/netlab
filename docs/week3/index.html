What I learned

Describe your design: why these routers, LANs and links, and how you chose the addresses.

The 4 routers with a triangle (r1, r2, r3) and r4 as a stub router connected to r1. The triangle gives the redundant path, and the stub gives a natural place for a summary. LAN numbers 1 to 4 is choosen so that only LANs 1 to 3 aligned to a /22 and LAN 4 is not included. On each link, the first router named is .1 and the second gets .2

Which route summarises several LANs? Which networks does it cover, why is it correct, and what else would it attract if you added a network inside its range?

The /22 on r4 (10.80.0.0/22) covers LANs 1, 2 and 3 but not LAN 4. It’s correct because all three LANs are reached through r1, and summary.txt shows all three using the same next hop, 10.80.14.1. If adding a network anywhere in 10.80.0.0 to 10.80.3.255 range, r4 would send its traffic to r1 whether or not it exists there.

When you took a link down, what happened, and why couldn't your routes recover even though another path existed? What would have recovered?

After link r1 r2 down, the trace from h1 to h2 stopped at r1 (10.80.1.1) with !N, also network is unreacheable. It couldn't take other routes because the static routes doesn't adapt, as it only know one path only. To recoverd a backup route via r3, a routing protocol (like OSPF) that notices the failure, or a controller.

In your network, what was the control plane? Who worked out the routes, and what does that mean for a network of 40 routers?

The user was the control plane. Every route was route by hand and wrote it into network.yaml With 40 routers, writing every route by hand is not practical because one mistake could cause a loop or unwanted errors. A routing protocol needed to be checked first.
